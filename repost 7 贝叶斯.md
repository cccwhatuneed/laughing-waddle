Bayes' Theorem: The Mathematical Foundation for Expanding Reasonableness from a Grounded Base
1. The Formula
Bayes' theorem is stated mathematically as:

text
P(A|B) = [P(B|A) × P(A)] / P(B)
Where:

P(A|B) is the posterior probability: the updated belief in hypothesis A after observing evidence B.

P(A) is the prior probability: the initial belief in hypothesis A before observing evidence B.

P(B|A) is the likelihood: the probability of observing evidence B if hypothesis A is true.

P(B) is the marginal probability: the total probability of observing evidence B under all possible hypotheses.

2. What It Means
Bayes' theorem describes the optimal way to update a belief when new evidence arrives. It is not an approximation. It is not a heuristic. It is a mathematical identity—derived directly from the definition of conditional probability—that holds under all conditions.

The theorem establishes that any rational system that begins with a prior belief and receives new evidence must update its belief in proportion to two factors:

How strongly the evidence supports the hypothesis (the likelihood)

How plausible the hypothesis was before the evidence arrived (the prior)

There is no other way to do this correctly. Bayes' theorem is not one possible approach. It is the only approach that is mathematically consistent.

3. What It Does
Bayes' theorem provides a formal mechanism for expanding the reach of reasonableness from a grounded base.

If you have a well-founded belief in one domain, and you encounter new evidence in a different domain, Bayes' theorem tells you exactly how to update your belief based on that evidence—without requiring you to "start over" or abandon your previous understanding.

This is the mathematical expression of cross-domain reasonableness: you do not need to re-derive everything from first principles in every new domain. You can extend what is already reasonable by incorporating new evidence as it arrives, while maintaining the consistency of your overall belief structure.

The theorem guarantees that this extension is not arbitrary. It is the unique update rule that preserves consistency with probability theory.

4. Why It Is a Foundation for Cross-Domain Reasonableness
Bayes' theorem has been verified across centuries of mathematical practice, from its formulation by Thomas Bayes in the 18th century to its extensive use in modern statistics, machine learning, signal processing, and scientific inference.

It is not a cultural preference. It is not a philosophical stance. It is a theorem—a proven consequence of the axioms of probability.

When you apply Bayes' theorem, you are not adopting a method. You are following a mathematical necessity. Any system that updates beliefs rationally must conform to this structure, regardless of whether the system is human, artificial, or natural.

This is why the theorem can serve as a foundation for cross-domain reasonableness: it is domain-neutral. It does not depend on the content of the beliefs or the nature of the evidence. It only depends on the structure of the relationship between beliefs and evidence.

6. A Note on Groundedness
The theorem itself does not tell you what to believe initially. It only tells you how to update your beliefs once you have evidence.

The initial belief (the prior) can be grounded in any well-founded source—observation, experiment, physical law, or structural recognition. Bayes' theorem then ensures that as new evidence arrives—from any domain, at any scale—the belief structure updates in a way that is mathematically consistent with both the prior and the evidence.

This is the mechanism by which a grounded base expands into reasonableness across domains: the base provides the starting point, and Bayes' theorem provides the update rule that keeps the expansion consistent with both the base and the new evidence.
